Common mistakes

link vs website

rightwrong

Find out more at [LINK, i.e. website address].

Find out more on the Helsana website.

Find out more under/on [LINK].

Find out more at the Helsana website.

Explanation:

  • A specific link is an address and thus takes “at”, just as a residential address would. By referring to a website as a whole, you’re providing a location.
    Example: He lives at 2 Broadmoor Street. She lives somewhere on Greene Road.

it when referring to a brand or company

rightwrong
To keep up with its changing audience, Helsana (= the company) rebranded in 2014.To keep up with their changing audience, Helsana rebranded in 2014.

Explanation:

  • In English we don’t identify a brand or company as a person but as a thing. Therefore don’t write “he/she”, “him/her” or “his/her” but “it” (subject/object) or “its” (possessive adjective).

affect vs effect

rightwrong

The decision will affect him greatly.

The decision will have no effect on your benefits.

The decision will effect him greatly.

The decision will have no affect on your benefits.

Explanation:

  • affect (verb): to change or influence someone or something
  • effect (noun): the result of a particular influence

assure vs insure vs ensure

rightwrong
  • I assure you that he’s good at his job.
  • Ensure you’re free when I visit next weekend.
  • I insure my car because the law requires it.

I insure / ensure you that he’s good at his job.

Assure / Insure you’re free when I visit next weekend.

I assure / ensure my car because the law requires it.

Explanation

All of these words have to do with “making an outcome sure,” which is why they’re frequently mixed up. However, they aren’t interchangeable.

  • assure (verb): to promise or say with confidence
  • ensure (verb): to make certain
  • insure (verb): protect against risk by regularly paying an

lose vs loose

rightwrong
  • The business is losing money.
  • This button is loose.
  • The business is loosing money.
  • This button is lose.

Explanation

  • lose (verb): to stop having something; not to make profit
  • loose (adjective): not tight, not firmly stretched or fixed, detached

agenda vs diary

rightwrong
  • I’m afraid my diary is full for this week, but I could see you next Monday.
  • The next item on the agenda is the publicity budget.
  • I’m afraid my agenda is full for this week, but I could see you next Monday.
  • The next item on the diary is the publicity budget.

Explanation

  • agenda (noun): a list of items to be discussed at a meeting; a list of aims or possible future achievements
  • diary (noun), also appointment book (meaning 1): a book with spaces for each day of the year in which you write down future arrangements
  • diary (noun) (meaning 2): a book in which you can write down the events that occur each day. I kept a diary of the project.

actually vs currently

right

  • It's unclear what actually happened that night.
  • She is currently working in London.

wrong

  • It's unclear what currently happened that night.

Explanation

  • actually (adverb): in fact or really
  • currently (adverb): at the present time
